Coming from pyribs or QDax

pyribs and QDax are the two main quality-diversity libraries in Python. This package offers most of their algorithms, under the names below, and checks several of them against those libraries on the same inputs (test/reference; see Verification).

Where the libraries differ in a rule, such as how a batch is inserted or how ties are broken, the docstring of the type says which rule this package follows and why.

Differences in approach

  • Genotypes. pyribs and QDax store solutions as arrays of numbers. Here a solution can be any Julia type, such as a graph or a molecule, with a vary method for it; see the graph tutorial.
  • Fitness is maximized, as in both libraries.
  • One loop. An algorithm is an archive and a tuple of emitters, run by solve or one round at a time with step!.
  • Randomness. Every function that draws random numbers takes the generator, and a run is reproducible from its seed, also with several threads.

Speed

benchmark/compare runs the same problems in all three libraries: MAP-Elites, CMA-ME and CMA-MAE on Rastrigin with 20 parameters and a 100 × 100 grid, MOME, and MAP-Elites and CMA-ME on a GPU. Its README gives the settings, the data of every run and how to reproduce them. In short, on one CPU core:

  • MAP-Elites is 2 to 14 times faster than pyribs, most at small batches. It is faster than QDax at batches of 100 and below; at 1000 and above QDax, compiled with JAX and with a grid lookup in place of its centroid search, is 1.2 to 1.35 times slower in float64 and 1.3 to 1.7 times faster in float32;
  • CMA-ME is 5 to 7 times faster than pyribs and CMA-MAE 6 times;
  • on a GPU at a batch of 10 000, MAP-Elites with a grid is level with QDax in float32 and 1.35 times slower in float64; with a CVT archive QDax is 2.7 times faster in float32 and 2.5 times slower in float64.

The objective in these benchmarks takes nanoseconds, so the numbers measure the library. With an expensive objective the evaluation dominates and the libraries differ little.
